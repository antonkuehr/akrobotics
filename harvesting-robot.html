Autonomous harvesting robot

A comparison of ToF scanning and camera-based object detection on an indoor plastic-ball harvesting rover, following earlier work on rover, arm and gripper control.

Comparison period
04/2024 – 09/2024 6 mos
Context

Retrieval time: 50.9 → 2.8 s/m in the indoor comparison across 18 target positions.

Finding and collecting a target

Before a harvesting robot can collect anything, it has to find a target and approach it in a position the gripper can reach. We explored that sequence on an indoor rover, using plastic balls as stand-ins for apples. Combining a mobile base, robotic arm and gripper allowed us to compare how two sensing approaches affected the complete retrieval task.

During the second and third semesters of my bachelor’s degree, from April 2023 to March 2024, I worked in a six-person team in Engineering Office II and III at TH Köln to develop the initial platform. This included the rover, arm and gripper control and a scanning distance sensor for finding targets.

The later comparison, from April to September 2024, built on that work. Denis Tolkovets and I compared the existing scanning approach with camera-based object detection. My responsibilities covered software and hardware architecture and computer-vision performance optimisation, including development and deployment of the camera pipeline on a Raspberry Pi 5. The platform and comparison were joint work.

Comparing two ways of finding the balls

The original system used a time-of-flight sensor, which estimates distance from the travel time of emitted light. A servo swept the VL53L1X sensor across the scene, allowing the rover to search for targets through a sequence of range measurements.

The camera approach used images to detect the balls and locate their centres. We trained a YOLOv8n object detector on 2,445 self-collected images and ran it on the Raspberry Pi 5. Its output included the detected class, confidence, image coordinates and colour.

The diagram below follows both sensing paths. They supplied different types of target information, but both connected to the rover’s approach and collection process. This allowed us to compare how the complete retrieval task performed with each sensing method.

Connecting perception to the rover

In the camera version, images reached the Raspberry Pi through a ribbon cable. The Pi processed them and sent target information over a serial connection to an Arduino Mega 2560. The Arduino handled target heading and distance, together with the commands for driving and grasping.

Front-wheel encoders supplied motion feedback, and the Arduino also controlled the cooling fans. The signal diagram shows how information passed from the camera through processing and control to the wheel motors and arm.

The rover’s 4000 mAh battery supplied the 12 V motor path through a Sabertooth controller. A converter provided 5 V for the Raspberry Pi, camera, Arduino and downstream hardware, with a low-battery warning threshold of 11.4 V. The power diagram shows these supply paths and their voltages.

Comparing retrieval performance

We compared the two implementations across 18 target positions on the same indoor track. The measurement covered the complete retrieval process: finding the target, approaching it and collecting it. We expressed retrieval time per metre to relate the result to the target’s distance from the rover.

Mean retrieval time was 50.9 seconds per metre for ToF scanning and 2.8 seconds per metre for camera detection. In this comparison, the camera version completed retrieval in approximately one eighteenth of the time per metre. The table below summarises the timing and observed task failures.

We recorded 12 failures in 30 ToF attempts and none in 18 camera attempts. The trial counts differed, so the percentages should be read together with those counts. No failures in the camera trials describes this set of observations; it does not establish failure-free operation more generally.

Retrieval times at all 18 target positions

The detailed heatmaps show how retrieval time varied with target position. Rows indicate distance from the rover, while the three coloured columns represent targets to the left, on the centre line and to the right. The side positions are 0.5 m from the centre line.

Each coloured cell contains a retrieval time in seconds. Both maps use the same colour scale, with darker green indicating a longer time. The final column gives the mean time for each distance divided by that distance, expressed in seconds per metre.

What the comparison established

The camera-based implementation completed the retrieval task more quickly and had fewer observed failures in the tested indoor setup. These results concern the complete sensing and collection implementations, rather than the sensor hardware alone.

The experiment used plastic balls on an indoor track. It did not establish performance with real fruit, plants or changing outdoor conditions. The side-by-side video below shows how the two implementations found and collected a target in the demonstration environment.
