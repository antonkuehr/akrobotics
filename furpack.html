The Furpack

A purpose-built transport backpack developed through mockups, sewn prototypes and supplier samples.

Project period
07/2025 – 04/2026 10 mos
Context
Prototyping & Concepting team at Furhat Robotics
Status
Design finalised; manufacturer confirmation pending
My contribution
Project leadership and collaborative backpack design
In collaboration with
Jonathan Majberger Lutz Lucas Gustavsson
Guidance by:
Nicholas Pugin

Physical prototypes, supplier samples and techpacks. Production tentatively expected to begin in early 2027.

Motivation and timeline

Researchers and Furhat Robotics employees often take Furhat to events, customer visits and research projects. Taking Furhat to another location means carrying the robot together with the equipment needed to use it. The robot’s face needs protection, while its power supply, microphone and laptop need to stay organised and accessible. Interviews with three colleagues highlighted the bulk of the existing transport cases and the difficulty of carrying a complete setup alone.

This need prompted the concepting team to develop a purpose-built way to transport Furhat. Jonathan Majberger Lutz and I began with two weeks of brainstorming and prototyping, working together through the custom-sewn prototype. I then continued independently, preparing a techpack, contacting potential manufacturers and requesting samples. The techpack brought together design requirements and construction details so manufacturers could make prototypes. Over the following months, supplier discussions and feedback from transport trials guided further refinements to the functionality and design.

As my time at Furhat came to an end, I handed the project over to Lucas Gustavsson, who had helped me finalise the design during the preceding months. He subsequently completed the remaining design work. The project now awaits final manufacturer confirmation and contract negotiations, with production tentatively expected to begin in early 2027.

Creating a backpack from scratch

We began by mapping how people would use the bag. Morphological charts helped us combine alternative solutions for each part of the design, while a dominance matrix helped compare design directions against our priorities. Sketches and prototypes then helped us explore how Furhat would fit inside a backpack and how someone would load and carry it. Each prototype focused on a different part of that question.

Paper mockups

Paper and tape offered a quick way to explore the outer shape, lids, pockets and zipper paths. The small-scale model helped us consider the overall form, while the larger mockup allowed a closer look at the fit around the robot itself. The photographs below show the small-scale model and the larger mockup with its opening closed and accessible.

Modifying an existing backpack

We then modified an existing backpack to investigate padding and support around the robot’s face, neck and body. This let us explore how a bag with an established carrying structure could hold the robot. The photographs below show the fitted padding and Furhat loaded into the modified backpack.

Sewing our own prototype

Our final in-house prototype brought these ideas together in a custom-sewn bag made from curtain fabric, cardboard padding and simple zippers. It allowed us to load the robot, close the bag and try transporting it, but also exposed problems with the padding and zippers. At 420 × 300 × 265 mm, it slightly exceeded our hand-luggage size target, which was a soft constraint. The photographs below show this prototype open and closed.

Developing the design with manufacturers

I created a techpack to turn our initial prototype ideas into a clear brief for manufacturers. It brought together the design requirements and construction details, providing a basis for samples and discussions about revisions. As testing revealed areas for improvement, supplier-specific versions documented the requested changes.

View the techpack

External prototyping followed two routes: direct manufacturers who could develop and produce the bag to the project’s requirements, and established backpack brands offering the possibility of a wider collaboration. Both involved discussions about materials, construction and functionality, but direct manufacturing was my main focus.

Direct manufacturers

I worked with four manufacturers based in South-East Asia, each making several prototypes to the project’s requirements. This route combined sample development with the option of subsequent mass production, with one of these manufacturers intended to produce the final Furpack.

Sample comparisons covered loading, shape retention, fabric, zippers, handles and carrying adjustments. Some externally made prototypes improved the shape but remained bulky or lacked reinforcement. The photographs below show a supplier sample’s fitted robot compartment and exterior.

Potential brand collaborations

In parallel, I contacted well-known Nordic backpack brands about collaborations that could combine manufacturing with shared branding and marketing. Among them was the Stockholm-based company Sandqvist. Its CEO got in touch, and I met him at Furhat’s office to discuss a possible collaboration. He brought a first prototype that Sandqvist had made from my techpack, giving us a concrete starting point for discussing the design and a potential partnership.

What we learned from the prototypes

Lucas joined at this stage, and we continued refining the design together. The direct-manufacturer samples and brand prototypes made further user testing easier and provided a broader range of feedback. To check water resistance, we put each prototype in the shower and inspected the inside afterwards. These observations and design discussions shaped the next set of requirements and supplier-specific techpacks. The table below shows some of the problems and proposed fixes.

Problems and proposed fixes identified through the prototypes.
Problem Fix
The robot’s forehead could catch on the opening during loading and removal. Requested a compartment that widened towards the opening.
The robot and removable mask needed secure retention and support. Specified head and neck support and retention for both the robot and mask.
Some samples lacked structural reinforcement. Requested thicker structural padding to help the bag retain its shape.
The load-lifter attachment caused shoulder discomfort. Proposed improved shoulder and lower-back padding.
Zipper seams could catch in the slider path. Requested smoother seams and less restrictive zipper covers.
The base needed more grip and stiffness. Proposed a stiff, non-slip bottom and higher-friction base pads.
The loaded bag needed a practical way to be lifted. Proposed a two-point top handle.
The laptop needed accessible, supported storage. Requested side access and a suspended laptop sleeve.
Small accessories needed to remain accessible in different bag positions. Proposed angled accessory nets for use with the bag upright or laid down.
