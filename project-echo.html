Skin for Humanoid Echo

Investigating scalable whole-body tactile sensing and a compliant protective skin for RSA’s student-led humanoid at TU Delft.

Project period
09/2026 – present 2 mos
Context
Extracurricular engineering project in RSA at TU Delft
Status
Team recruiting, research and concept evaluation
My contribution
Research and Skin Team Lead
Guidance by:
Wolf Lenssens

Context

Project Echo grew out of the 2025 RSA RoboArm competition. Wolf Lenssens founded the project with the goal of building an open-source humanoid. It is currently the only project at TU Delft pursuing this goal and is part of the Robotics Student Association (RSA). The project is organised into Hand, Arm & Torso, and Embedded departments, with more than 15 members and growing further. Its shared target is to take part in RoboCup Rescue in April 2029, a competition testing robots in simulated disaster-response scenarios.

Starting a skin team

Looking for a hands-on humanoid project at TU Delft, I came across Project Echo. When Wolf saw the Robotic Tactile Skin project I had worked on during my bachelor’s, he came up with the idea of adding a skin department to the project. So I started by exploring the question of what skin even means for a humanoid: reading papers, brainstorming and discussing ideas with professors at TU Delft.

I realised that compared to building a humanoid arm or hand there are no all-in-one solutions for skin. Even within the RoboCup use case requirements, the designs could go in many independent directions. Should the skin protect against impacts, debris, water, heat or chemicals? Should it detect contact and location, or also normal and shear forces? How much detail is needed for safe contact with people, and should the outer surface cover the mechanics or shape the robot? For rough terrain, a robust cover, such as a leather jacket, and impedance control might do the job. For interaction with people, a soft surface and detailed, distributed touch sensing could matter more.

Sensing and material investigation

To get an overview of what is already out there, I am exploring different ways of giving robots a sense of touch. I have come across everything from robots punching walls to find their way around a room without any other sensors, as in TapNav, to researchers trying to bring the movie hero Baymax closer to life with an inflatable skin that senses contact from inside using ToF sensors. At TU Delft, I also had the chance to talk to Michaël Wiertlewski about ShadowTac, which uses a camera to read how a soft membrane deforms when touched. Alongside these research projects, I am looking at commercialised options such as iCub’s capacitive skin. The research moodboard below shows those and more projects I came across during my search.

Two additional approaches I find particularly interesting for Echo's use case and resources are eFlesh and conformal electrical impedance tomography (EIT) skin. eFlesh uses a soft, 3D-printed TPU structure with small magnets embedded in it. When the surface is pressed, the structure deforms and the magnets move. Magnetic sensors underneath measure the resulting field changes; after calibration, these readings can be used to estimate contact position and force. EIT instead uses a flexible, electrically conductive TPU sheet. When it is touched, conductive patches make contact with the sheet and change the voltages measured around its edge. Software then reconstructs where the contact occurred. Both would let us start with small printed prototypes and explore how their shape and softness could be adapted to Echo.

Current status and next steps

The next phase focuses on obtaining physical samples and growing the team. I plan to approach sensor suppliers and researchers for samples or student kits, ideally with the readout hardware, software and documentation needed to try them. Recruitment is intended to bring in people with different expertise and ideas around materials, design and electronics.

The target for the first project evening on 3 December 2026 is to have samples that I can show others to spark new ideas and to show what skin could be useful for. Hopefully, by then, the skin team will have grown so that we can start prototyping. If sample outreach and recruitment do not lead to a workable path, the fallback is a plan to build a prototype myself using one of the two detection methods explained at the end of the last section.

That prototype would start with contact detection on a small area before considering curved coverage. A later step could display touched regions during teleoperation and record contact data to explore its usefulness for manipulation tasks. Regular project updates are shared on Project Echo’s Instagram.
