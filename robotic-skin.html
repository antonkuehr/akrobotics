Robotic Tactile Skin

A completed feasibility study of an experimentally evaluated 8 × 8 textile tactile sensor matrix placed on a forearm.

Project period
10/2024 – 03/2025 6 mos
Context
BSc course: Product Engineering II at TH Köln
Status
Completed feasibility study
My contribution
Collaborative sensor development and evaluation
In collaboration with
Fabio Jain
Guidance by:
Stefan Benke

64 sensing locations and two discrete activation thresholds, demonstrated in a flexible BSc contact-matrix prototype.

Giving a surface a sense of touch

I believe tactile sensing is important if robots are to become part of everyday life. Fabio Jain and I explored that idea through a prototype for Product Engineering II during our bachelor’s degree at TH Köln. The aim was to distinguish lighter from stronger contact using materials and fabrication methods available in a university workshop. We investigated a flexible textile sensor that could be placed on a forearm, demonstrating the sensing principle and evaluating its behaviour.

Detecting contact through layered fabric

Our approach used conductive tracks arranged across several layers. One set of tracks runs perpendicular to the others, forming an 8 × 8 matrix with 64 sensing locations. Elastic separators keep the tracks apart until pressure compresses the material enough for them to touch through openings in the separator.

A sender layer sits between two receiver layers, with a separator on each side. Under light contact, the separator with the lower triggering threshold compresses enough for the sender to touch one receiver layer. Stronger contact also compresses the other separator enough to connect the sender to the second receiver layer. Together with the untouched state, these connections distinguish two contact levels; they do not provide a continuous force measurement. An Arduino activates the sender tracks one at a time and checks which receiver tracks are connected, locating contact from the active sender–receiver combination. Making the sensing layers

We sewed Madeira HC-12 conductive thread onto denim in zigzag tracks. Denim provided a fabric that could be sewn and resisted tearing, while the conductive thread allowed us to build the electrical pattern without specialised printing or plating. The photograph below shows these sewn tracks.

Each track was 5 mm wide, with 8 mm between track centres. We used laser-perforated EVA foam between the sensing layers. Changing its thickness and fill density—the proportion of solid foam left after perforation—changed how much pressure was needed to bring the conductors into contact.

The prototype’s material costs were below €10. This was a material-cost figure for the prototype, excluding labour and the cost of an installed system.

Reading the contact matrix

Reading all of the tracks directly would require many microcontroller connections. We used shift registers to activate the sender tracks and read the receiver states through fewer connections. The diagrams below show how the SN74HC595 output registers selected senders and how the SN74HC165 input registers collected receiver states.

For each scan step, one sender is active while the receiver states are sampled. Pull-down resistors and protection diodes supported the circuit.

This sequential scan reduced the connections needed at the microcontroller, but scanning and physical wiring still presented challenges for larger sensing areas. We did not measure the frame rate or establish whether multiple simultaneous contacts could always be read without false detections.

The communication protocol reserved a third active level for possible future extension, but the prototype implemented two.

Testing contact and flexibility

We tested where contact was detected, how the separator affected the activation threshold, and whether the sensor still worked when curved. The table below summarises the setup and observation for each test.

Contact and flexibility tests on the textile-sensor prototype.
Test Setup Observation
Contact detection Pressed the sensing surface, including larger contact areas. Contact was detected across all 64 sensing cells, with several cells responding to a larger press.
Activation thresholds A 16 mm test cube with rounded edges; five tests averaged for each configuration. Increasing separator fill density and thickness from 2 to 3 mm raised the pressure needed for activation. The investigated range was approximately 10–100 Pa; threshold values were rounded to reflect measurement uncertainty.
Flexibility Wrapped the sensor around a 100 mm-diameter cylinder. The sensor remained functional, with no measurable change in activation behaviour in that test.

What the prototype established

The study demonstrated that sewn conductive tracks and elastic separators could form a flexible sensor for locating contact and distinguishing two activation levels. The video below presents the prototype and its sensing principle.

Remaining limitations

  • The 8 mm track spacing describes the layout; it is not a validated localisation accuracy.
  • Precise triggering accuracy was not established, and material deformation produced measurement errors of up to 10%.
  • The complete stack was more than 1 cm thick, and wiring became burdensome as the sensing area increased.
  • Maximum bending, long-term durability and whole-body coverage remained untested.

This was a completed feasibility study rather than a fully developed robotic skin.
