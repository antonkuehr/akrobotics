Furhat’s audio system

A redesign of Furhat’s microphone integration, evaluated for its effect on speech recognition.

Project period
01/2026 – 04/2026 4 mos
Context
Bachelor’s thesis at Furhat Robotics and TH Köln
Status
Prototype built and evaluated
My contribution
Thesis and project were conducted only by me.

Mean word error rate: 18.3% → 16.6% across 480 paired runs. A 9.3% relative reduction under the tested conditions.

Context and starting point

Furhat is a social robot designed to interact with people through conversation. For that interaction to work, its microphones need to capture speech clearly enough for the recognition software to understand it. The way a microphone is installed inside the robot is part of that process: sound has to travel through an opening in the shell before reaching the sensor.

For my bachelor’s thesis at Furhat Robotics and TH Köln, I investigated whether changing that mechanical integration could improve speech recognition. The existing microphone circuit board, connection to the robot and outer shell had to remain unchanged. Within those constraints, I carried out the research, developed the mount, built the prototype and evaluated its performance.

Rethinking the microphone mount

I started by examining how sound reached the microphone in the existing assembly. Foam surrounding the circuit board formed a cavity in front of the sensor and could partly obstruct its inlet. The assembly was also supported by the loudspeaker housing, creating a possible path for unwanted vibration.

My redesign gave sound a short, aligned channel from the shell opening to the microphone. I attached the assembly to the shell and used a thin silicone seal around the channel entrance. A second part held the circuit board in compressed closed-cell foam, while a plug closed the enclosure and supported the cable routing. The photographs below show the original arrangement and the completed redesign.

The intention was to create a more controlled sound path and reduce unwanted coupling. The finished assembly still contained rigid connections, however, so it did not fully isolate the microphone from the surrounding structure.

Turning the design into a prototype

The small space beside the loudspeaker made fabrication an important part of the design process. Resin printing reproduced the acoustic features more successfully than the available filament printer, but thin walls could break or fail to print. These constraints led to a final channel measuring 1.6 mm in diameter and 3.1 mm in length.

Printing the part at an angle also distorted the channel’s cross-section. I adjusted the opening in CAD to obtain an approximately circular printed passage and routed the wiring downwards to fit the assembly beside the loudspeaker. I also explored silicone casting, but abandoned it after difficulties with removing the parts from the mould and producing the thin walls.

A simplified acoustic model placed the channel’s fundamental resonance above 20 kHz. This was a design estimate; I did not measure the prototype’s frequency response.

Testing speech recognition

To find out whether the redesign helped, I compared the original and modified assemblies while they recorded the same speech simultaneously. The robot’s internal recording interface was mono, so I developed and evaluated one microphone side.

I automated speech playback, recognition requests through Furhat’s interface, and collection of the resulting Google Cloud Speech-to-Text transcripts. The test used one male British speaker’s recording of ten Harvard sentences, repeated under four noise conditions. There were 120 paired runs per condition, giving 480 paired comparisons of the same speech material.

The conditions ranged from 20 to 5 dB signal-to-noise ratio, a measure of speech level relative to background noise. Lower values mean more noise relative to speech. The 20 dB condition used no added noise; the others used a fixed recording of background babble. The photograph below shows the meeting-room setup.

I assessed recognition using word error rate, which counts substituted, omitted and extra words relative to the reference transcript. Lower values indicate fewer recognition errors.

What the tests showed

Across all four conditions, mean word error rate fell from 18.3% to 16.6%: a reduction of 1.7 percentage points, or 9.3% relative to the original assembly. The table below shows the results for each condition. One-sided paired t-tests indicated an improvement at p < 0.05 in all four.

Mean word error rate (lower is better). 120 paired runs per SNR condition; 480 overall. Rounded values.
SNR Original A Redesign B
20 dB 21.4% 17.2%
15 dB 16.6% 15.5%
10 dB 16.7% 16.1%
5 dB 18.8% 17.8%
All four conditions 18.3% 16.6%

The result supported the redesigned assembly under these test conditions. Because I changed several features together, the experiment did not establish how much each individual change contributed. Evidence for reduced internal-noise coupling remained qualitative.

I also tested an external ReSpeaker microphone as a reference. It achieved 9.5% mean word error rate at 5 dB, but only 60 runs were usable. Its position approximately 30 cm closer to the sound source and different signal chain prevented a direct comparison of microphone mounting alone.

The study was limited to one room and one speech recording, and measured transcription errors rather than conversational success. I also suspected transcript-boundary artefacts in the recording interface. Furhat’s microphones were muted while it spoke, so recognition during robot speech and acoustic echo cancellation were not evaluated. The outcome was a built and evaluated prototype; production adoption was outside the thesis scope.
