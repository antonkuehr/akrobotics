Furhat’s audio system

A redesign of Furhat’s microphone integration, evaluated for its effect on speech recognition.

Project period
01/2026 – 04/2026 4 mos
Context
Bachelor’s thesis at Furhat Robotics and TH Köln
Status
Prototype built and evaluated
My contribution
Thesis and project were conducted only by me.
Guidance by:
Nicholas Pugin Stefan Benke

Mean word error rate: 18.3% → 16.6% across 480 paired runs. A 9.3% relative reduction under the tested conditions.

Context and starting point

Furhat is a social robot designed to interact with people through conversation. For that interaction to work, its microphones need to capture speech clearly enough for the recognition software to understand it. The way a microphone is installed inside the robot is part of that process: sound has to travel through an opening in the shell before reaching the sensor.

For my bachelor’s thesis at Furhat Robotics and TH Köln, I investigated whether changing that mechanical integration could improve speech recognition. The existing microphone circuit board, connection to the robot and outer shell had to remain unchanged. Within those constraints, I carried out the research, developed the mount, built the prototype and evaluated its performance. The formal thesis lasted nine weeks, with submission in March 2026. The January–April project period also includes preparation before the formal thesis, followed by the defence and project wrap-up in April.

Read the thesis

Rethinking the microphone mount

I started by examining how sound reached the microphone in the existing assembly. Foam surrounding the circuit board formed a cavity in front of the sensor and could partly obstruct its inlet. The assembly was also supported by the loudspeaker housing, creating a possible path for unwanted vibration. The photographs below show the foam around the circuit board and the original assembly mounted inside Furhat, beneath its outer moulded shell.

To develop a new concept I read scientific resources, looked at technical guidelines and implementations of other robots and microphone systems. Additionally I held expert interviews to assess the specific challenges I would face for Furhat's implementation. Consolidating these findings gave me a clearly defined concept for the new mount. It has a short, aligned acoustic channel to guide sound from the shell opening to the microphone. It is attached to the shell, with a thin silicone seal around the channel entrance and uses compressed closed-cell foam supporting the circuit board. The aim was to create a more controlled sound path and reduce unwanted coupling. A simplified acoustic model placed the channel’s fundamental resonance above 20 kHz which is well above the human voice frequency range. The sketch below shows the final concept in top, side and front views.

Turning the design into a prototype

Turning the concept into a working prototype meant reproducing the small acoustic channel within the space available inside Furhat. I first tried fused deposition modelling (FDM), which builds parts from melted plastic filament, but the available printer struggled with the small features. I then tried silicone moulding; the thin walls were difficult to produce, and removing the parts from the mould was challenging. Switching to more precise resin printing brought its own initial problems: thin walls could break or fail to print, while printing at an angle distorted the channel’s cross-section.

By iterating on the geometry, channel dimensions and print settings, I eventually produced resin-printed parts accurate and robust enough to assemble and test. The final channel measured 1.6 mm in diameter and 3.1 mm in length and fitted inside Furhat. The assembly consisted of Mount A, which formed the acoustic channel and attached to the shell with a thin silicone seal around the entrance, and Mount B, which held the circuit board in compressed closed-cell foam. A plug closed the enclosure and supported the cable routing. Rigid connections remained, so the assembly did not fully isolate the microphone from the surrounding structure. The photographs below show how these parts came together, from the shell attachment and foam lining to the installed circuit board and completed assembly.

Testing speech recognition

Furhat used only one microphone for speech analysis, so I developed and evaluated the mount on that side. To find out whether the redesign helped, I compared the original and modified assemblies under the same test setup:

  • Both assemblies recorded the same speech simultaneously.
  • I automated speech playback, recognition requests through Furhat’s interface, and collection of Google Cloud Speech-to-Text transcripts.
  • The speech material was one male British speaker’s recording of ten Harvard sentences.
  • Each condition included 120 paired runs, giving 480 paired comparisons of the same speech material in total.

I repeated the recording under four signal-to-noise ratios (SNR), which describe the speech level relative to background noise. Lower values mean more noise relative to speech:

  • 20 dB SNR: no added noise.
  • 15 dB SNR: added background babble.
  • 10 dB SNR: added background babble.
  • 5 dB SNR: added background babble.

The three added-noise conditions used the same fixed recording of background babble. The photograph below shows the meeting-room setup.

I assessed recognition using word error rate, which counts substituted, omitted and extra words relative to the reference transcript. Lower values indicate fewer recognition errors.

What the tests showed

Across all four conditions, mean word error rate fell from 18.3% to 16.6%: a reduction of 1.7 percentage points, or 9.3% relative to the original assembly. The table below shows the results for each condition. One-sided paired t-tests indicated an improvement at p < 0.05 in all four.

Mean word error rate (lower is better). 120 paired runs per SNR condition; 480 overall. Rounded values.
SNR Original A Redesign B
20 dB 21.4% 17.2%
15 dB 16.6% 15.5%
10 dB 16.7% 16.1%
5 dB 18.8% 17.8%
All four conditions 18.3% 16.6%

The redesigned assembly had a lower mean error rate in each tested condition. However, errors did not simply increase as the background noise increased: the original assembly had its highest mean error rate in the 20 dB condition, with no added noise. These tests did not establish why that happened.

I also tested an external ReSpeaker microphone as a reference. At 5 dB, it achieved a mean word error rate of 9.5%, compared with 17.8% for the redesigned assembly. The ReSpeaker was approximately 30 cm closer to the sound source and used a different signal chain, so the comparison does not isolate the effect of the microphone mount but gives an impression how much room to improvement remains.

Alongside the built and evaluated prototype, I handed over research findings and recommendations that could inform a future hardware redesign of Furhat’s audio system.

Limitations

  • Several mechanical features changed together, so the experiment did not establish how much each individual change contributed.
  • Evidence for reduced internal-noise coupling remained qualitative.
  • The study used one room and one speech recording, and measured transcription errors rather than conversational success.
  • Transcript-boundary artefacts were suspected in the recording interface.
  • Furhat’s microphones were muted while it spoke, so recognition during robot speech and acoustic echo cancellation were not evaluated.
